# Flow, fairness and failure lens

Covers how challenge evolves against skill over time, whether players read outcomes as fair and learnable, what failure costs, and which friction helps or obstructs.

## When to use

- Players report boredom, overwhelm or a sudden wall.
- Feedback says "unfair", "rigged", "cheap" or "random".
- Players quit after a loss rather than retrying.
- A death, reset, penalty or loss mechanic is being tuned.
- Progress slows and nobody knows whether it is the challenge or the obstacles around it.

## Gather

- A timeline of the experience: opening minute, first session, first competence, mid-game, late game, re-entry after a break.
- Difficulty and tuning data, enemy or level lists, drop rates and odds.
- What happens on failure: what is lost, where the player restarts, how long until they are playing again.
- Playtest observations of quits, rage moments and retries.

## Checks

### Flow over time

1. For each phase, compare what the game demands with what the player can do by then. Flow needs challenge slightly ahead of skill (Csikszentmihalyi).
2. Split skill into axes: execution, tactics, strategy, system knowledge, attention, coordination. A phase that suddenly tests a new axis while raising an old one is a spike.
3. Look for plateaus (no new demand, so boredom) and cliffs (several demands jump at once, so anxiety).
4. Is there a rhythm of tension and relief, or does difficulty only ramp?
5. Does the player see their own improvement before the next ramp?

### Attribution

Weiner's attribution theory asks where players place the cause of an outcome.

1. After a failure, will players blame something internal and changeable ("I dodged too late") or something external and fixed ("the game cheats")?
2. Was the threat telegraphed, readable and avoidable with the information available?
3. Does the failure screen or replay show the cause?
4. Is success credited to the player, or does it feel handed out?

### Perceived randomness

1. Is randomness applied before the decision (input: a map, a hand of cards, which invites planning) or after it (output: a hit roll, which can override skill)?
2. How do streaks feel? Players see patterns in randomness; three misses at 90% reads as rigged even when it is honest.
3. Are odds shown, and do they behave the way players intuitively expect?
4. Is there bad-luck protection for long droughts on important rewards?
5. Can randomness erase several minutes of skilled play? That is a trust risk.

### Failure loop

1. What does a failure cost: time, progress, resources, rank, social standing? Is that cost proportional to the stakes and the session length?
2. What does the player learn from the failure, and how clearly?
3. How long from failing to meaningful play again? Long reloads, unskippable scenes and walk-backs turn practice into punishment.
4. What emotion does the post-failure moment produce: "one more try" or "I'm done"?
5. Does repeated failure lead somewhere: assists, alternative routes, partial progress?

### Friction

1. Separate productive friction (the challenge itself, meaningful scarcity) from obstructive friction (menus, waits, grind, unclear goals, inventory limits).
2. Map where friction stacks: several obstacles in a row with no reward between them.
3. Is any friction there mainly to sell its removal? Flag it as an ethics finding.

## Common problems

- **Difficulty cliff**: several new demands arrive in the same encounter.
- **Plateau**: hours of play with nothing new to master.
- **Opaque death**: the player cannot tell what killed them or how to avoid it.
- **Output dice on skill moments**: a roll overrides a well-executed play.
- **Retry tax**: the time to replay dwarfs the challenge being practiced.
- **Stacked obstruction**: grind, gates and waits back to back.

## Fix menu

- Introduce one new demand at a time and give it a safe practice beat.
- Add a relief beat after each spike.
- Telegraph threats earlier and show the cause on the failure screen.
- Move randomness to inputs, smooth streaks, or add drought protection.
- Cut time-to-retry with checkpoints, instant restarts or skippable scenes.
- Remove the single worst obstruction in the stack, rather than adding a reward to compensate.

## Reporting notes

- Present flow as a phase table: phase, demand, expected skill, state (flow, boredom or anxiety).
- Quote the exact encounter, roll or screen behind each fairness finding.
- Treat perceived fairness as Assumed unless backed by player feedback.
