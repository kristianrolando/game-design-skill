# Player motivation lens

Covers why players start, keep going and come back: the needs a design feeds or starves, the quality of its rewards, who it suits, and whether a specific feature gets used.

## When to use

- Players finish content but do not care, or stop once rewards dry up.
- A feature ships and few players use it.
- The team cannot say who the game is for, or lists everyone.
- Reward systems keep growing to prop up engagement.
- A design may suit one audience and quietly repel another.

## Gather

- The intended audience, in the team's words, and any player data or survey results.
- The reward structure: currencies, unlocks, cosmetics, ranks, story beats.
- Choices the player makes and how much they matter.
- Social touchpoints, if any.
- For a feature question: where and when the feature is offered, and what using it costs the player.

## Checks

### Psychological needs

Self-Determination Theory (Deci and Ryan) names three needs that strongly predict enjoyment and persistence.

1. **Autonomy**: are choices meaningful and self-chosen, or is the player herded? Count the forced steps between choices.
2. **Competence**: can players see themselves improving? Is feedback clear enough to learn from?
3. **Relatedness**: does the player feel connected to characters, other players or a community?
4. For each need, note where the design feeds it and where it undermines it, for example timers that remove choice or unexplained failures that block learning.

### Reward quality

1. Sort the main drivers: the activity is enjoyable in itself; it expresses identity or values; it earns status; it pays an external reward.
2. Would the core activity still be worth doing with rewards removed? If not, the loop leans on payouts.
3. Are rewards informational ("you got better") or controlling ("do this or lose that")? Controlling rewards erode intrinsic interest over time.
4. Does reward inflation appear: bigger numbers needed to produce the same feeling?

### Player types and fit

1. Pick a taxonomy that matches the game. Bartle's achievers, explorers, socializers and killers suit online worlds. Finer motivation models (for example action, social, mastery, achievement, immersion and creativity clusters) suit broader audits.
2. For each relevant type: served, neglected or harmed? A system that delights competitors can drive away explorers in the same shared space.
3. Personality fit (Big Five) when relevant: novelty and ambiguity (openness), structure and maintenance load (conscientiousness), social intensity (extraversion), conflict (agreeableness), stress and loss pressure (neuroticism).

### Persona and anti-persona

1. Derive the ideal player from what the design rewards: session length, skill demand, social appetite, tolerance for loss and grind.
2. Write the anti-persona: who will bounce, and at what moment. Knowing who leaves, and when, usually guides cuts better than the persona does.
3. Compare with the audience the team claims. A gap is a finding.

### Feature adoption

Use the Fogg Behavior Model: a behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment.

1. **Motivation**: why would the player want this, right then?
2. **Ability**: how many steps, how much time, money, thought or social risk does it take?
3. **Prompt**: is the player prompted at a moment of high motivation and ability, or during combat, a loss or a loading screen?
4. If adoption is low, find which of the three is missing before adding rewards.

## Common problems

- **Reward crutch**: the activity is not enjoyable, so payouts keep growing.
- **Choice theatre**: options that never change outcomes.
- **Invisible competence**: players improve but nothing shows it.
- **Audience of everyone**: no anti-persona, so every feature tries to please all types.
- **Harmful mix**: one type's preferred play damages another's in a shared space.
- **Bad prompt timing**: features offered when the player is busy, frustrated or leaving.

## Fix menu

- Add or clarify one meaningful choice per loop cycle.
- Make improvement visible: personal bests, mastery tiers, before and after comparisons.
- Replace a controlling reward with an informational one.
- Write the anti-persona and use it to cut or segregate features.
- Move a feature prompt to a calm, high-motivation moment and remove one step from using it.

## Reporting notes

- Report needs as fed or undermined, each tied to a specific mechanic.
- Do not equate a taxonomy label with a real player; say which behaviors define the type here.
- Treat adoption diagnoses as Inferred until funnel or playtest data exists.
