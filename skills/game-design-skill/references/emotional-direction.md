# Emotional direction lens

Covers the emotional promise: who the player gets to be, what they should feel, and whether every layer of the game supports that.

## When to use

- The pitch names a theme or genre but not what the player feels or becomes.
- Art, audio, mechanics and UI copy point in different emotional directions.
- The team argues about whether a feature "fits" and has no shared test.
- Playtesters call the game fine but forgettable.
- An event, season or set piece needs a clear mood before production.

## Gather

- The one-line pitch and the comparable games the team names.
- The verbs the player performs most often, and the verbs the marketing emphasizes.
- Art and audio references, moodboards or a style guide, if any.
- Key moments: the first reveal, the climax of a typical session, the end of a session, level or campaign.
- Playtest quotes about feelings, if available.

## Checks

### Player fantasy

1. Complete "I am a ___ who ___ so that ___" using only what the mechanics let the player do. Compare the result with the fantasy the team claims.
2. Are the fantasy's key verbs in the core loop, or only in cutscenes, store text and trailers?
3. Does the fantasy grow? Compare how it plays in minute five with hour ten.
4. Is it specific? If it could be pasted into a rival game unchanged, it is not yet this game's fantasy.

### Emotional target

1. Name two or three target emotions and one emotion to avoid. "Fun" and "epic" do not count; use words such as tension, curiosity, mastery, coziness, dread, mischief, pride.
2. For each target emotion, name the system that produces it. An emotion with no producing system is a wish.
3. Is there contrast between rest and intensity? Constant intensity numbs; constant calm drifts into boredom.

### Tone alignment

1. Check art, audio, animation, UI copy, mechanics and monetization prompts against the targets. Each mismatch is a finding, for example a cozy farm interrupted by aggressive countdown offers.
2. Does feedback weight match action weight? A heavy hit with a light sound reads as weak.

### Memorable moments

1. Which moment would a player describe to a friend? If nobody can name one, flag it.
2. Where is the high point of a typical session, and how does the session end: on a high, on an open question, or on a chore such as sorting inventory, an ad or a loss screen?
3. After a failure, does the closing beat invite a retry or a quit?

### Novelty and ambition

1. What is familiar (lowers learning cost) and what is new (gives a reason to pick this game)? Too much of either is a risk.
2. If the concept feels safe, describe the bolder version of its fantasy in one sentence, then the cheapest way to test whether that version is worth it.

## Common problems

- **Borrowed fantasy**: the emotional promise belongs to a comparable game; nothing is specific to this one.
- **Wrapper-only theme**: the theme lives in story and art while the mechanics stay generic.
- **Emotion without a source**: a target emotion that no system produces.
- **Tonal whiplash**: monetization, UI or difficulty undercuts the intended mood.
- **Flat session shape**: no designed peak, and sessions end on chores or losses.
- **Novelty imbalance**: everything is new at once, or nothing is new beyond the skin.

## Fix menu

- Rewrite the fantasy as verbs and move at least one of those verbs into the core loop.
- Choose one primary emotion; reskin or cut features that fight it.
- Design one peak per session and move chores away from the session's end.
- Keep controls and conventions familiar and concentrate novelty in one system.
- Write a short tone guide: three target emotions, three "never" rules, three reference moments.

## Reporting notes

- Put the extracted fantasy in one sentence next to the claimed one.
- Tie each tone mismatch to a named asset, screen or system.
- Mark emotional claims as Assumed unless playtest quotes support them.
