# Pitch and GDD lens

Covers communicating a design: writing a one-page design document, checking whether a game design document is healthy, and reviewing a pitch deck.

## When to use

- Turning rough notes into a compact concept document.
- A GDD is long, outdated or ignored by the team.
- A pitch deck is going to a publisher, investor, platform or funding program.
- Stakeholders keep misunderstanding what the game is.

## Gather

- Notes, existing docs or deck slides; read them in full.
- The audience for the document: the team, a publisher, an investor, a grant jury.
- The stage and the decision the document should support.
- Any submission requirements, such as a page limit, template or required sections.

## Checks

### One-page design document

A one-page document should let a newcomer explain the game after a single read. Cover these sections, in roughly this order, and omit any the stage cannot answer yet, listing it under open questions instead:

1. Title and a one-sentence logline.
2. Genre, platform and input, target player.
3. Player fantasy.
4. Core loop, as an arrow chain.
5. Three to five pillars, written as trade-offs.
6. Three to five key features, each tied to a loop step or pillar.
7. Progression and meta, in one or two lines.
8. Art and audio direction, with references.
9. Comparable games and the main difference from them.
10. Scope: team, target milestone, rough timeframe.
11. Open questions and biggest risks.

Density rules: every line must help someone decide or understand; no lore before gameplay; no feature that is not linked to the loop or a pillar.

### GDD health

1. Is there one source of truth, or several conflicting docs?
2. Does it record decisions and their reasons, not only descriptions?
3. Does each feature have a goal and an observable acceptance criterion?
4. Do the loop and pillars come before content lists?
5. Which sections are stale compared with the current build?
6. Can someone find the answer to a common question in under a minute?

### Pitch deck

1. **Order**: hook and fantasy, gameplay (shown early, ideally with images or video), what makes it different, audience and comparables, team credibility, progress and proof, scope with budget and timeline, the ask.
2. **Dream and proof**: is ambition balanced by evidence, such as a playable build, playtest results, wishlists, a prior shipped game?
3. **The ask**: is it explicit: how much, for what, over what period, and what the partner gets?
4. **Fit**: does the game match the recipient's portfolio, platform and deal size?
5. **Readability**: one idea per slide, readable text size, a deck that survives being read without a presenter.
6. **Credibility**: are budget and timeline plausible for the scope? Cross-check with the scoping lens.

## Common problems

- **Lore first**: pages of world and story before any gameplay.
- **Feature list without a loop**: the reader cannot picture minute-to-minute play.
- **Undecided doc**: describes options but never states the decision.
- **Stale GDD**: the build has moved on and the doc has not.
- **Hidden ask**: the deck never says what it wants.
- **Dream without proof**: big promises, nothing playable or measured.
- **Mismatched comparables**: references to hits far outside the team's scope.

## Fix menu

- Collapse the concept into the one-page structure and move everything else to appendices.
- Put a gameplay image or clip on the second slide or the first page.
- Add a decision log with dates and reasons.
- Add one acceptance criterion per feature.
- Write the ask as a single sentence and put it on its own slide.
- Replace an aspirational comparable with one the team's scope can realistically match.

## Reporting notes

- For a one-page document request, put the document under Deliverable, then list gaps as findings.
- For deck reviews, reference findings by slide number.
- Never invent market sizes, sales figures or wishlist counts; mark missing proof as a gap.
